How long does it take to learn coding?

Answered in hours, because "three months" means nothing until you know how many hours a day those three months contain.

The honest answer: about 150–200 hours to be comfortable with one language's basics, and 600–1,000 hours to be hireable as a junior with projects you can show. Anyone quoting a single month or a single year is guessing about you. Hours are the only number you control.

Milestones in hours

These assume practice that includes typing code and being wrong, not video-watching. A learner with prior maths or spreadsheet experience sits at the low end; someone starting from a blank terminal at the high end.

MilestoneHoursWhat "there" looks like
Comfortable with basics150–200You write loops, dicts and functions without looking anything up, and read simple error messages without panic.
Automate your boring work200–300You read a spreadsheet or a folder of files, transform it, write the result, and handle the rows that break.
Finish a small real project300–450Something with other users or other files: a site, a tool, a bot. You have hit the boring parts and shipped anyway.
Interview-ready junior600–1,000Projects you can explain decisions on, plus common data-structure questions in one language.
Independent at work2,000+You estimate, debug other people's code, and know which questions to stop asking.

Estimates from how long these milestones typically take, not a guarantee for one person. The spread between people is mostly about what they do when stuck, not how fast they read.

The same hours on a calendar

Consistency beats duration. Six days a week at half an hour beats a Sunday marathon that happens twice a month — because the marathon is the one that does not happen.

Read it the other way: if your goal is a job, 30 minutes a day is a five-year plan, and that is a legitimate choice — as long as you chose it knowing the arithmetic. Most people who say "I do not have time to learn to code" actually mean "I do not have four hours a day", and then quit the half hour too.

Why the hours stretch

Six months of tutorials, zero programs

Watching someone code teaches vocabulary, not skill. If in the last week you typed nothing that could fail, your hours are not counting.

Restarting instead of finishing

Switching language or framework every time a project gets hard resets you to the beginner tier while feeling like progress. Pick one and go boring.

Being stuck in silence

A missing semicolon costs four hours only when nobody is asked about it. Getting help fast is the biggest speed-up available, and the one beginners avoid most.

No gate on what counts as done

"I learned loops" is uncheckable. "I wrote FizzBuzz from memory and it ran first try" is checkable, and tells you where you actually are.

The shortest version that still works

  1. Pick one language for the next 90 days. If you are unsure, Python — it has the least setup between question and answer.
  2. Fix a daily slot, small enough that you never negotiate it.
  3. Follow an order, and write code inside every session.
  4. After 100 hours, build one thing for yourself, and finish it.
  5. Ask the moment you are stuck past twenty minutes.
